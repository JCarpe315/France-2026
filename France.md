## KEY FRENCH TERMS USED THROUGHOUT

A quick reference, so no reader needs to stop and search. Each term is also explained where it first appears in the story.

- **Assemblée nationale / député** — the lower house of parliament and its members (like a congressman).
- **Sénat / sénateur** — the upper house, elected indirectly; members are senators.
- **Président / Premier ministre** — the president (elected by all voters) and the prime minister (appointed by the president, answerable to parliament).
- **Département / commune / région** — France's three local-government layers: county, town, region.
- **Décret / arrêté** — two levels of government regulation below a law: a decree (national) and an order (ministerial or local).
- **Préfet / préfecture** — the state's appointed representative in each département, and their office.
- **Lycée** — the public high school; its students are *lycéens*.
- **Bourse** — the state student grant.
- **Ehpad** — *établissement d'hébergement pour personnes âgées dépendantes*: a state-regulated nursing home.
- **SMIC** — the statutory minimum wage.
- **49.3** — the constitutional clause letting a government pass a bill without a vote.
- **Motion de censure** — the no-confidence motion that can topple a government.
- **PLF / PLFSS** — the annual budget bill and the social-security financing bill.
- **LPM** — the multi-year military programming law.
- **RIP** — the referendum of shared initiative: a citizen-demanded vote on a law.
- **QPC** — the priority question of constitutionality: a citizen route to challenge laws before the Constitutional Council.
- **CJIP** — the *convention judiciaire d'intérêt public*: the negotiated settlement that lets a company pay its way out of prosecution.
- **Mise en examen** — formal charging (placement under investigation) by an investigating judge.
- **Garde à vue** — police custody.
- **Témoin assisté** — "assisted witness": a status between witness and suspect.
- **Détournement de fonds publics** — embezzlement of public money; **blanchiment** — money laundering; **recel** — receiving stolen goods; **trafic d'influence** — influence-peddling; **prise illégale d'intérêts** — illegal conflict of interest.
- **Pantouflage** — the revolving door between senior public office and private industry.
- **Grands corps** — the small elite schools-and-corps system that staffs the state's summit.
- **OQTF** — *obligation de quitter le territoire français*: a deportation order.
- **Parrainage** — a mayor's or elected official's sponsorship, required to run for president.
- **Front républicain** — the traditional closing of ranks against the far right in a runoff.
- **Casseurs** — the "breakers": rioters who loot and torch amid protests.
- **Reste-à-charge** — what a patient pays out of pocket after insurance.
- **Restos / CROUS** — student restaurants; the CROUS is the state student-services agency.

# PART ONE — THE MACHINE

## 1.1 The Fifth Republic: a system built for a man, still running on his design

France's current constitution dates to 1958. It was written for Charles de Gaulle to end the paralysis of the Fourth Republic, which had burned through twenty-one governments in twelve years — an average of seven months per government, each falling the moment its parliamentary coalition frayed. The Fourth Republic's flaw was pure parliamentarism: governments served at the pleasure of the assembly, and the assembly had no stable majority. The Fifth Republic's designers solved this not by fixing parliament but by sidelining it. The result is a hybrid — a presidential system wearing a parliamentary costume — whose stability depended on one thing: a president who commands a majority in the National Assembly. French constitutionalists call this the *fait majoritaire*, the majority fact. Part Two of this document is the story of what happens when that fact disappears; nothing in the constitutional text changed, and yet, in the words of the constitutional scholar cited there, "the France changed regimes."

**The presidency.** The president is elected directly by the people for five years — the *quinquennat*, reduced from seven by the 2000 referendum specifically so presidential and legislative terms would coincide and the *fait majoritaire* would reproduce itself automatically. The office commands the armed forces, appoints the prime minister (Article 8), negotiates and ratifies treaties, can dissolve the National Assembly (Article 12), can submit referendums to the people (Article 11), and — under Article 16 — can assume emergency powers when the republic's institutions, independence, or international commitments are threatened in a grave and immediate manner. Article 16 has been invoked exactly once: from April 23 to September 29, 1961, during the Algiers generals' putsch, when de Gaulle ruled by special powers, created special tribunals, censored, and dismissed officials at will. The Conseil d'État of the day refused to review his acts. A 2008 constitutional revision finally leashed the article: the Constitutional Council must now review the conditions of its use at thirty and sixty days.

**The prime minister and the government.** The prime minister "directs the action of the government" and is responsible before the Assembly (Article 20) — the only genuinely parliamentary feature of the system. In normal times, the president sets direction and the prime minister runs the state. In *cohabitation* — when the president loses the assembly — the roles invert, and the prime minister becomes, in effect, the head of the executive. France has lived through three cohabitations (1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002); what has happened since 2024 is not cohabitation but something the text never contemplated: no majority at all, for anyone.

**Parliament, deliberately weakened.** The National Assembly has 577 deputies elected for five years by two-round majoritarian vote in single-member constituencies — a system that manufactures majorities from pluralities and punishes third parties. The Senate's 348 senators are elected indirectly, mostly by an electoral college of local officials; its composition structurally overrepresents rural and small-town France, which is why it has been a conservative fortress for most of the Fifth Republic and why, as of September 2026, it remains the only institution the far right does not threaten. Article 34 defines what law may cover — everything else is left to government regulation by decree (Article 37), which is why French governments govern without parliament far more than most democracies can. The 2008 constitutional revision did add genuine powers: the opposition controls committee chairs and inquiry commissions proportionally, and any law can be challenged after promulgation by the *question prioritaire de constitutionnalité* (QPC), a citizen-originated filter routed through the Conseil d'État to the Constitutional Council.

**The system's tools, in ascending order of drama:**

- **Article 49.3** allows the government to pass a bill without a vote unless a censure motion carries within twenty-four hours. The tally through February 2025: 115 uses since 1958. Michel Rocard used it 28 times (1988–91, governing with no majority — the historical precedent for today); Élisabeth Borne 23 times in twenty months, the per-time record, including the 100th use of all on March 16, 2023, for the pension reform. Barnier used it once — fatally. Bayrou used it twice in one day (February 5, 2025: budget and social security, surviving with 128 and 122 censure votes, six Socialist rebels defying their party to abstain). Lecornu used it once (January 20, 2026, on the revenue half of the 2026 budget) after pledging publicly he would not. Only once in the Fifth Republic has a censure motion succeeded — against Pompidou in 1962, after which de Gaulle dissolved and won a bigger majority. The never-users include Jospin, Fillon, Ayrault, Chaban-Delmas, Messmer and Couve de Murville.
- **Censure motions.** More than 115 tabled across the republic's history; the success rate is one. Borne faced roughly thirty of them, the modern record — each one a day of parliamentary theater that costs the government nothing and the country something.
- **Dissolution** (Article 12). The president may dissolve the Assembly and call elections. Used in 1962, 1968, 1981, 1988, and 1997 — the last of these — Chirac's 1997 dissolution, which produced five years of cohabitation against his design — and then, on the night of June 9, 2024, by Emmanuel Macron. Everything in Part Two flows from that evening.
- **Article 16** emergency powers (detailed above) and the 1955 *état d'urgence* statute: used in Algeria in 1955 and 1958, for one day in Wallis in 1987, in New Caledonia in 1984–85 after the assassination of the Kanak leader Machoro and in 1988 after Ouvéa; proclaimed by Chirac on November 8, 2005 during the banlieue riots, validated by the Conseil d'État against an open letter from 74 law professors, and extended three months; proclaimed by Hollande on November 14, 2015 after the Bataclan, extended six times until November 1, 2017 — the longest state of emergency ever, under which 1.6 million home visits and thousands of house arrests preceded the SILT law absorbing the powers. The March 23, 2020 sanitary emergency decree belongs to the same family. On October 19, 2026, curfews were ordered without any of these instruments — by ordinary prefectural police power, a first in the crisis (Pattern Fourteen).
- **The Constitutional Council** — nine members, nine-year terms, one-third appointed by each of the three presidents (republic, assembly, senate). Since March 8, 2025, its president is Richard Ferrand, elected by a single vote — the former Socialist-turned-macronist who shepherded Macron's 2017 legislative program as Assembly president. The current college: Ferrand flanked by Bas, Vichnievsky, Gourault, Séners, Malbec, Mézard, Pillet, Juppé, with Aurélie Bretonneau as secretary general. The workload has exploded: 81 decisions in 2025 (55 QPCs) against 42 in 2024, with 26 *saisines* — a fifteen-year record — and 11 more in the first half of 2026. The institution is now simultaneously attacked by the left, the right, and the far right, and the jurists' own review calls the new college "accordée à la droitisation de la France" — attuned to the country's rightward turn — while noting Fabius had pushed Barnier's justice minister Migaud for his own seat in vain. The late-quinquennat placement pattern — Ferrand at the Palais-Royal, Montchalin at the Rue Cambon (see 2.3), Guillaume at the Conseil d'État — the Élysée defends as "sécuriser les institutions."
- **The Conseil d'État**, supreme administrative court and the government's legal adviser, led since May 21, 2026 by Marc Guillaume — "the first fonctionnaire of the Republic" — an ENA graduate (promotion Victor Hugo, 1991), legal chief of the Defence Ministry 1995–2002, Justice civil-affairs director 2002–2007, secretary general of the Constitutional Council through the Debré era 2007–2015, Élysée secretary general under Hollande then Macron 2015–2020, and prefect of Paris through the Olympics and the Seine cleanup 2020–2026, co-author of a standard constitutional-law reference. He carries two shadows preserved in the record: the 2020 accusations of sexism from Élysée advisers, and his Sciences Po board resignation in the Duhamel affair.
- **The Conseil supérieur de la magistrature**, the judges' council, where the president sits ex officio — a structural fact that mattered in the Clearstream affair, when a sitting president was partie civile in a case touching his own interests while presiding over the judges' council (Part Three).

**The deeper machinery: the grands corps.** Beneath the elected layer, France is run by a few hundred graduates of a handful of schools — the ENA (renamed INSP in 2022), the Inspection des Finances, the Cour des comptes corps, Polytechnique — who circulate for entire careers between ministries, ministerial cabinets, the Élysée, the banks and industry, returning to their corps whenever politics ends. The Conseil constitutionnel's own constitutional-law literature states the mechanism plainly: of the roughly 700 *très hauts fonctionnaires* of the central decision milieu — Grémion's term — only a fraction is discretionary, and "le ministre choisit presque toujours des personnes issues des grands corps," both because the corps press to keep the posts in the family and because the minister needs the network. The detachment regime enables the endless ping-pong; France matches Germany, the Netherlands and Belgium in imposing no limit whatsoever on civil servants holding political mandates. Gremion's *Profession: décideurs* remains the standard account. The practical consequence runs through every part of this document: ministers change; the *décideurs* remain; and the same family names keep reappearing across the money chapters.

## 1.2 Who runs the country today (October 2026)

**President Emmanuel Macron**, in office since May 14, 2017, re-elected April 24, 2022 with 58.54% against Marine Le Pen's 41.46% — 18.7 million votes against 13.3 million, a margin of 5.5 million, down from 10 million in 2017 (66.10/33.90, 20.7 million votes). The 2022 geography registered the upheaval: Le Pen topped 18,000 communes, carried 23 metropolitan departments against just Aisne and Pas-de-Calais in 2017, and flipped Martinique (60%) and Guadeloupe (69.6%) five years after Macron's 77.55% Martinique score. Abstention was 28.01% with 2.2 million blank ballots. The Montaigne institute measured the *front républicain*'s erosion: LFI voters went 42% Macron, 24% abstention, 17% blank. Constitutionally barred from a third consecutive term (Article 6 of the 2008 revision), his mandate ends May 13, 2027. His endgame is a live mystery, documented in full in 2.3.

**Prime minister Sébastien Lecornu**, in office since October 10, 2025 — the fourth man to hold the office in fourteen months. The biography, from the official record: born June 11, 1986, in Eaubonne (Val-d'Oise), son of an aircraft technician and a medical secretary, grandson of a resistance fighter; schooled at Saint-Adjutor in Vernon, with an interlude among the Benedictines of Saint-Wandrille; a law degree and a public-law master's at Panthéon-Assas. ## 1.3 The debt: the number underneath everything

Every thread in this document ends, eventually, at the same figure. The fiscal ladder: deficits of 4.7% (2021), 5.4% (2022), 5.8% (2023), 5.1% (2024, revised), and an official 2025 target of 5.4% that came in at 5.1% — a rare upside surprise announced as a March 27, 2026 INSEE surprise, with the prime minister's "quoi qu'il arrive" and his "avec prudence" musing about sub-5% for 2026. Debt: **€3,460.5 billion at end-2025 — 115.6% of GDP** (INSEE), of which the state held €3,067bn, social security €287bn, local government €109bn; real-time ticking at €3,638.9 billion (119.8%) on October 13, 2026. Interest charges: **€64.7 billion in 2025, up 11.2% year-on-year** — 2.2% of GDP, more than the entire defense budget, heading to €74.5 billion (the "engagements financiers" mission) by 2027. Compulsory levies reached **43.6% of GDP in 2025** — the heaviest ratio in decades and among the OECD's highest. The interest line has passed the defense line and is closing on education.

The 2026 borrowing program: **€310 billion** of net medium- and long-term issuance — a record, roughly 10% of GDP, confirmed December 30, 2025, made possible only by the special budget law of December 27, 2025 that let the state borrow from January despite having no budget. The mechanics (AFT): new 3-year, 5/6-year and 10-year benchmark lines; indexed bonds ≈10% of net issuance; buybacks as conditions allow.

The market's verdict is priced every day. On October 19, 2026, the 10-year OAT yielded **4.88%** (+10bp in a session, +41bp in a month, +152bp in a year), against Germany's ~3.42% — a spread of ~137 basis points, down from the October 2 peak of 146.9bp and up from 80bp on August 27, 2025 (the Bayrou-crisis print). The spread's full history tells the regime story: a record peak of 225.1bp on November 17, 2011 (the sovereign crisis), negative -2bp on January 20, 2005, near-zero averages before the euro, 30–50bp the benign band, 65–85bp the post-summer-2024 plateau. The frame from the analysts: France repriced from "quasi-core" to "intermediate" issuer; the Bank of Singapore's October 13, 2026 warning — "OATs on the edge."

**Who holds the debt:** the negotiable state debt is ~56–57.5% non-resident (57.5% at T1 2026 per the AFT; 55.9% end-2025 per the Banque de France on long-term debt; ~50% on Maastricht-wide debt per the IMF). Trajectory: 28% in 1999 (pre-euro) → 70.6% in 2009 → 47.9% at the 2021 QE peak → 53.2% end-2023 → 57.5% T1 2026. Anatomy of the foreign holders: 38% investment funds, 22% foreign central banks, 25% insurers/pension funds, 15% hedge funds and others, routed chiefly through Japan, the US, the UK and Germany with large transits through Luxembourg and Irish fund domiciles. Resident side: insurers ~9.6–9.8%, banks 10.5%, the Banque de France for the ECB ~20.6% (€550bn of PEPP/PSPP holdings ≈ 15% of Maastricht debt), OPCVM 1.7–1.8%. The instrument skew: BTFs 81.3% non-resident, inflation-linked OATs 85.5% resident. G7 frame (September 2025): France 52% foreign-held vs Germany 45%, Spain 43%, Italy 28%, Japan ~10%. The Senate's September 24, 2025 report (r24-902) holds both truths: diversification is confidence; geopolitical dependence is risk.

**The ratings chronicle:** downgrades by Fitch, S&P and Moody's across 2024–2025, each memorandum citing the trajectory rather than the level — and the market now prices France as the eurozone's defining fiscal question.

# PART TWO — THE CHAIN OF FALLEN GOVERNMENTS (2024–2026)

## 2.1 June 9, 2024: the bet that broke the machine

The evening of June 9, 2024. The European Parliament election results in France, official: turnout 51.85% (a record for a European contest). The Rassemblement National: 31.37% and 30 seats. Ensemble: 14.60% and 13 seats. Socialists–Place Publique: 13.83% and 13. La France insoumise: 9.89% and 9. Les Républicains: 7.25% and 6. Les Écologistes 5.5% and 5; Reconquête 5.47% and 5. The far right in its two parties near 40% combined. The cohort inversion that explains the next two years: the 18–24 vote went LFI 33%, RN 25%.

Hours later, Macron dissolved the National Assembly — the fifth use of Article 12 after 1962, 1968, 1981, 1988 and 1997, and the first since Chirac's 1997 dissolution, which produced five years of cohabitation against his design. Macron's calculation, per every reconstruction: a snap election would either restore a working majority or force the country to confront the far-right threat at legislative scale. The first round on June 30 delivered the tripolar Assembly: RN 29.2%, the New Popular Front (the hurried left alliance: LFI, Socialists, Ecologists, Communists) 28.0%, Ensemble 20.0%, LR 6.6%. The second round on July 7, after the NFP and Ensemble withdrew candidates in three-way races, produced the hung parliament: NFP plurality (~180 seats), Ensemble (~168), RN and allies (~143), Republicans (~39). Three blocs, none near 289, none able to govern or to be governed.

The premiership conveyor belt that followed, measured precisely:

- **Gabriel Attal** (January–September 2024), the youngest prime minister of the Fifth Republic at 34, handed over.
- **Michel Barnier** (September 2024): 73 years old, a Gaullist of forty years' standing, the EU's former Brexit negotiator. Appointed after a 67-day government-formation lag — the longest of the Fifth Republic. He served 3 months and 18 days. His budget, carried by Article 49.3 on October 23, 2024, fell to a censure motion on December 4, 2024: 331 votes, the first time in the Fifth Republic a government had been brought down by censure. The RN sealed it; Mélenchon called it "the end of an era"; the markets took note.
- **François Bayrou** (December 13, 2024): the MoDem leader, three times presidential candidate, installed at Matignon after extracting from Macron the promise of a "banque de la démocratie" — a public campaign-financing bank that was the price of his 2017 endorsement and that has never been created. He served 8 months and 17 days. His premiership survived censure motions on February 10, February 19, June 4 and July 1, 2025 — each time on the tacit neutrality of the PS and the RN, each time buying weeks. Then, on August 25, 2025, he staked everything on an Article 49.2 confidence vote over his €43.8 billion savings plan (deficit target 4.6%, two public holidays cut): the Assembly withdrew its confidence on September 8, 2025 — 364 against, 194 for, 15 abstentions. The first-ever Fifth Republic government felled by a confidence vote. The RN voted the censure; Le Pen demanded "ultra-rapid dissolution"; Macron refused.
- **Sébastien Lecornu I** (October 5–6, 2025): announced on a Sunday evening with a partial roster — Borne reconducted at Education, Le Maire at the Armed Forces, Valls at Overseas, Woerth at Housing, Montchalin at Public Accounts, Moutchou at AI. That same Sunday evening, interior minister nominee Bruno Retailleau resigned on X: the government "ne reflète pas la rupture." By Monday the markets had fallen (CAC 40 -1.7%, banks -4/-5%), the Castex-to-SNCF nomination was suspended, Mélenchon demanded the destitution motion (104 signatures) be examined, and Le Pen called dissolution "incontournable" and a Macron resignation "wise." Macron's ultimatum — "ultimes négociations" by Wednesday — produced a reappointment. Lecornu I lasted one month and one day, the shortest premiership of the Fifth Republic.
- **Sébastien Lecornu II** (October 13, 2025 — present): 34 ministers, 211 of 577 deputies behind him at the outset, six LR ministers suspended from their party on October 23, 2025, Darmanin definitively withdrawing from Renaissance on October 14. A first mini-reshuffle announced February 7, 2026, decreed February 26, 2026 to adjust for ministerial candidacies (Dati's Paris race, Ferrari, Fournier, Rist); a second reshuffle September 18–22, 2026 when Dominique Faure left for the Association des maires de France. As of today Lecornu II has survived a year and nine days — governing without a single further 49.3, by arithmetic, attrition, and the special-law era's strange mercy: parliament refused the 2026 budget outright, and the state continued anyway.

The structural reading, documented in Part Five: without a *fait majoritaire*, the Fifth Republic's tools — 49.3, dissolution, even the confidence vote — become instruments of survival rather than governance. "Sans qu'une seule virgule de la Constitution ait été modifiée, la France a changé de régime" (Morel, 2025): the end of the majority fact converted rationalized parliamentarism into permanent crisis management, and each tool's use feeds the next crisis.

## 2.2 The budget war: 2025–2027, year by year

**The Barnier budget (autumn 2024).** The proximate cause of his fall: a 2025 budget built on €6 billion of "contribution exceptionnelle" on large companies and €4 billion of spending cuts. The RN, having extracted promises it judged unkept (heating-aid restoration, theRIC—citizen-initiated referendum—concession dangled), withdrew its tolerance; the left never offered any. ## 2.5 The overseas file: nuclear debt, nickel, and autonomy

**French Polynesia — the nuclear compensation war.** Between 1966 and 1996 France detonated 193 nuclear tests — the first seventeen in the Sahara (1960–66, in the lead-up to the Évian Accords), then 46 atmospheric and 147 underground in Polynesia, ending January 27, 1996 with the final shot, months after the September 1995 Papeete riots had greeted Chirac's resumption of testing. ## 3.1 Nicolas Sarkozy: three cases, one trajectory

**The Bygmalion case (2012 campaign accounting).** Sarkozy's 2012 re-election campaign exceeded its legal spending ceiling by roughly half, by having its official event supplier, the communications firm Bygmalion (owned by the friends of party treasurer Jean-François Copé), bill the UMP party rather than the campaign for €11.8 million of rally costs — fabrication costs, printing, staging — while the party financed the arrangement through fictitious invoices. Convicted May 2020 (one year, electronic tag); the conviction was definitively confirmed in December 2024, and Sarkozy wore the bracelet from February to May 2025 at his wife's Paris home — the first former president of the Fifth Republic to serve a criminal sentence. His co-defendants included Jérôme Lavrilleux (UMP deputy director, two years suspended), and Guy Allemand of Bygmalion.

**The Bismuth case (corruption and influence-peddling, 2014).** After losing his judicial immunity in July 2013, Sarkozy, as a private lawyer, offered his services to a senior judge, Gilbert Azibert of the Cour de cassation, via a second phone registered under the alias "Paul Bismuth" — bought by an aide in his own name — in exchange for inside information on an investigation touching the 2007 campaign finances. Convicted March 1, 2021: three years, one firm, tag; the cassation confirmed in December 2024. Azibert and Herzog were convicted alongside him.

**The Libya case (2007 campaign financing).** The paper trail, assembled by Mediapart from 2012 onward, by three investigating judges, and tested in two trials:

- A note signed by Libyan intelligence chief Moussa Koussa, dated December 2006, planning €50 million for Sarkozy, with a preparatory meeting in Tripoli on October 6, 2006 (Senoussi and Bachir Saleh for Libya; Hortefeux and Takieddine for France).
- The notebooks of banker Bachir Saleh, found by Dutch police after his death (he died in 2015; his notes surfaced in 2021), recording between the two rounds of the 2007 election: "sent €1.5M to Nicolas Sarkozy" and "€2M via Senoussi."
- Three transfers of €6.5 million into accounts linked to intermediary Ziad Takieddine in January and November 2006, plus a €500,000 transfer.
- Takieddine's testimony (2012, repeated in court; retracted 2020; re-repeated days before his death): €5 million carried in suitcases, €2 million to Sarkozy at the ministry of the interior, €2 million at avenue d'Iéna.
- The campaign's own ledger: €35,000 in unexplained cash at the end of the race, against €6.5 million of spending never explained by its declared resources.
- Guéant's money: an unexplained €500,000 arriving in 2008, a Patek Philippe Philippe watch from arms broker Alexandre Djouhri, laundering through a fake Flemish-painting sale to fund his apartment, and a villa sale to a sovereign fund directed by Kadhafi's right hand; the Swiss banker's testimony tracing laundering through Saudi cousins' accounts.

The first-instance court (September 25, 2025) found the *pact* proven — the tripartite Libyan financing agreement of October 2005, corroborated by Takieddine's 2005 travel records, the October 2006 meeting, and Senoussi's own contemporaneous notes — but not the money's arrival in the campaign, and sentenced Sarkozy to five years (two firm), with Guéant convicted alongside. The appeal, heard March 16–June 3, 2026, saw the defense unravel mid-trial: the 19,000-document USB-key defense, the retrocommissions thesis, and Guéant's April 14, 2026 attestation that Sarkozy personally ordered him to handle Senoussi's file. Prosecutors sought seven years and a €300,000 fine with a five-year ban on the final day. **Verdict: November 30, 2026.** The witness-subornation companion case — Sarkozy and Carla Bruni-Sarkozy indicted over Takieddine's 2020 retraction (the "Sauver Sarko" affair) — runs in parallel, with Mimi Marchand also charged.

**Collateral figures, named:** Claude Guéant (chief of staff; indicted; convicted in the first instance; ten years sought on appeal); Brice Hortefeux (interior minister; heard as a witness); Alexandre Djouhri (arms-linked intermediary, arrested at Heathrow in 2018, extradited, convicted in Paris in 2023 — money-laundering and witness-tampering, €500,000 fine); Bachir Saleh — Kadhafi's paymaster, exfiltrated from Libya by France in 2012 per the Canard enchaîné, granted protection and never interrogated in the case; Ziad Takieddine — dead in Beirut on September 23, 2025, two days before the verdict, aged 75 (see Pattern Two).

## 3.2 Marine Le Pen: the assistants case and the ineligibility earthquake

The scheme, as the courts established it: between 2004 and 2016, the Front National — renamed Rassemblement National in 2018 — used its European Parliament assistants, paid by the Parliament to work in Strasbourg or Brussels, to work for the party in France. Twenty-four accused plus the party itself; €2.9 million of fictitious contracts; €474,000 personally attributed to Le Pen; the party fined €2 million. The court called it a *système* — a systemic scheme. The origin: a Mediapart journalist embedded in FN circles from 2015, then a deliberate scan of all Strasbourg assistants' lists to test the systemic hypothesis across parties (the scan that also caught the MoDem and LFI files, below).

**The first sentence (March 31, 2025):** four years (two suspended, one firm under electronic tag), €100,000 fine, and **five years of ineligibility with provisional execution** — which would have removed her from the 2027 race on the spot. Aliot: 18 months (6 firm) with three years' ineligibility; Gollnisch: three years (one firm, bracelet) with five years *with* provisional execution.

**The appeal (July 7, 2026):** the Paris appeals court re-crafted the sentence — **45 months of ineligibility, of which 30 suspended, leaving 15 months deemed fully served since March 31, 2025** (the day of the first-instance conviction), on the court's reasoning, read from its own communiqué, that ignoring already-purged time would attack "la liberté des candidatures, condition essentielle à une expression démocratique du suffrage universel." The prison term (three years, two suspended, one firm — the year-long tag) was not given provisional execution: it attaches only if and when the conviction becomes definitive — "si elle fait campagne, elle débutera celle-ci sans bracelet," said the procureure générale. The fine: €100,000, unchanged. The parquet had sought four years (one firm) and five years' ineligibility *without* provisional execution — so on the appeal, the punishment of substance moved from the ineligibility to the tag, and the tag is suspended.

**The cassation (pending):** pourvoi filed July 21, 2026; the Cour de cassation's criminal chamber had already tightened the provisional-ineligibility doctrine twice (May 28 and September 30, 2026 rulings); the constitutional layer sits in QPCs 2025-1129 and 2025-1175; the rival "1993 revival thesis" for the first-instance ban circulates among the jurists. Deadlines reset October 16, 2026, and the cour has publicly committed to ruling **before the April 18, 2027 first round** — with the extraordinary escape hatch preserved in the record: if elected before the court rules, she would not have to comply with anything until her term ends. The suspension mechanics confirmed: her cassation filing put the appeal verdict, tag included, on hold.

**The political earthquake, itemized:** 8 million viewers for her TF1 interview after the first verdict; BRDP threats probed; Bayrou's "soutien inconditionnel" plus his opened door to a parliamentary look at provisional execution; Ciotti's suppressive PPL; Bardella's Romania-parallel "tyrannie des juges"; Mélenchon's "destitution should return to the people"; Tondelier's "justice is the same for everyone"; Zemmour's cross-factional defense; and the sentence ladder's context — the ineligibility precedent ladder: Balkany's ten years never lifted (November 2025) versus Cahuzac, Fillon and Le Pen's five (or 45 months). Bardella's arc: from emergency understudy (March 31, 2025, the post-verdict summit, Le Pen's "au RN nous avons la chance d'être deux") through the April 26, 2025 officialization ("si elle devait être empêchée demain..."), the June 25, 2025 Valeurs actuelles interview (Le Pen: "lui ai moi-même demandé de se préparer"), the July 7–8, 2026 reversal at La Flèche (Bardella "rétrogradé" to prospective Matignon, then reclaiming the candidacy within days, his Le Figaro pivot to insecurity: "La violence, c'est celle qui frappe les Français") — to the Sud Ouest epitaph: "Il était un remplaçant, il est devenu une alternative, aujourd'hui c'est le candidat naturel."

## 3.3 François Fillon: €900,000 of family, the €135,000 magazine, the €800,000 bill

Revealed by the Canard enchaîné on January 24, 2017, eleven weeks before a presidential election he was favored to win. The elements, as the courts established them: Penelope Fillon drew roughly €900,000–1 million gross over 1998–2013 — two parliamentary assistant contracts in her husband's office (the going rate, €3,800–7,900/month at thirteen months per year) plus a contract as an assistant to Fillon's *suppléant*, the cardiologist Joël Sarlot, in the Sarthe — with a striking scarcity of documentary evidence of actual work: no badge logs, no emails, colleagues' testimony of her rare presence, and her own 2007 interview to the Telegraph, resurfaced during the trial: she had "never been his assistant." Their children Marie and Charles drew €84,000 as assistants while trainee lawyers. And the *Revue des deux mondes*, owned by billionaire Marc Ladreit de Lacharrière, paid Penelope €135,000 gross for two reading notes (de Lacharrière took a plea deal in December 2018: eight months suspended, €375,000 — "de pure complaisance... satisfaire une demande de François Fillon").

The judicial ladder, step by step: convicted June 29, 2020 (five years, two firm, €375,000 fine, ten years' ineligibility); re-sentenced on appeal May 9, 2022 (four years, one firm, ten years' ineligibility upheld); the Cour de cassation partially annulled (February 2024) on the basis of the reassessment; retried June 17, 2025: four years suspended, €375,000 fine, five years' ineligibility, and €800,000 in damages to the Assemblée nationale (with €126,167 re-quantified for the 2012–13 Sarlot contract); the European Court of Human Rights rejected his appeal unanimously October 23, 2025 ("défaut manifeste de fondement"). In February 2026 he desisted; the conviction is now definitive. Repayment: ~€680,000 over fifteen years — "rendre l'argent." The still-open file: the PNF probe into the 2013–15 contract under which public funds allegedly paid his speechwriter Maël Renouard to write his presidential book *Faire*.

## 3.4 Rachida Dati: watches, retainers, and three concurrent proceedings

The Ghosn–Renault trial (September 16–28, 2026, verdict January 21, 2027). The facts as presented by the PNF: Dati, then a Member of the European Parliament, had been Renault's lawyer since 2010 at €15,000 a month via RNO Consult — a governance entity through which Carlos Ghosn and co-director Thierry Bolloré remunerated figures around Renault (~€900,000 total, of which ~€450,000 attributed to her personally) — while Ghosn's Renault simultaneously funded, through the same structure, a stream of gifts: Cartier, Bulgari and Chaumet watches and jewelry. Her ethics declarations: "néant" for items over €10,000; her declared patrimony €5.6 million, built on her lawyer income since 2010. Libération's exposé quantified the undeclared jewelry at ≥€420,000, from ex-partner Henri Proglio (ex-CEO of Veolia, then EDF — the revolving-door flag in one gift). In court: "Je ne me suis jamais plainte… je crois à la vie, au droit, à la justice." The prosecution's requisitions, on record: four years (two firm, bracelet), five years' ineligibility, five-year bars from public office and the bar, €450,000 fine, €900,000 confiscation.

**The second file:** the jewelry non-declaration inquiry (open). **The third:** opened December 18, 2025 (Libération's revelation; perquisitions included) — corruption suspicions over lobbying for GDF Suez during her lawyer years, which she "rejettes en bloc." She remains a declared LR presidential candidate; she lost the Paris mayoral race to Grégoire in March 2026; and she sat as justice minister through 2025 before the Lecornu II reshuffle carried her out of government — into her candidacy and her trials.

## 3.5 The Bolloré case: campaigns, ports, and the price of a plea

The case theory, as the judges retained it: in 2010–11, Havas (the communications group Vincent Bolloré controlled) under-invoiced its campaign services for Faure Gnassingbé's re-election in Togo and Alpha Condé's victory in Guinea; the port concessions followed — Togo Terminal's 35-year concession already signed February 14, 2011 (Conakry seized from Necotrans by government intervention; the Nanterre tribunal awarded Necotrans €2.1M on October 10, 2013, for completed works). The JEAN-PHILIPPE DORENT emails that investigators say confirm the suspicions; Alpha Condé's own quote in evidence: "C'est un ami, je privilégie les amis." Bolloré, his son Cyrille, and Dorent face up to ten years at trial.

The escalation ledger, fully dated: investigation opened 2013 → the February 2021 **CJIP of €12 million**, accepted by the group while Bolloré's own guilty plea was **refused** by the tribunal ("the facts too serious to escape a public debate") → June 2024 PNF requisitions for corruption and complicity of abuse of trust → the November 6, 2025 Cour d'appel arrêt confirming the referral with some pieces struck for presumption-of-innocence violations → **trial December 7–17, 2026**. On September 1, 2026, Le Monde revealed Bolloré had filed a criminal-conspiracy complaint against the judges.

The money math, on the record: Bolloré Africa Logistics = up to a quarter of group revenue and up to 80% of profits (per the RAF collective's pleading); sold to MSC in December 2022 for **€5.7 billion**; the €12M CJIP = roughly one-third of one percent of the sale price (Rosalux: "incredibly modest against €5.7 billion"). The restitution counter-offensive: eleven West African NGOs, led by Patrice Lumumba's great-nephew Jean-Jacques Lumumba, filed a March 18, 2025 complaint for *recel* (receiving) and *blanchiment* across five countries (Togo, Guinea, Ghana, Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire), demanding the €5.7bn be returned to the affected peoples under the 2021 restitution law; Antoine Vey is the collective's lawyer.

**The infrastructure ledger (primary sources):** the Lomé third quay — 300bn FCFA (~$640M/€458M) fully self-financed by BAL, launched March 4, 2011 by Gnassingbé with Bolloré present, built by Vinci (February 2012 contract), backlands by Eiffage (18bn FCFA, June 2013), operational end-2013; capacity 311,500 TEU (2013) → 1.9M TEU + 30M tonnes (2023), the world's 94th port; transhipment from 5% (2012) to ~64% of traffic (2019); port net projected +94% the year of commissioning. The concession constellation: Togo Terminal (35 years, 2010), Conakry, Dakar Roro (€97M, 25 years, November 2013), Abidjan (since 2004), Douala/Kribi, Tema. **The Françafrique personnel map:** Jacques Poignant — son of Bernard Poignant, Hollande's thirty-two-year friend — installed at Lomé under the "bienveillante" wing of Charles Debbasch (Gnassingbé's minister-adviser, the Vasarely-affairs convicted man, kept in place until 2012, the man Sarkozy had called a "mercenaire" in 2007); Bernard Poignant then organized Gnassingbé's 2013 Lorient/Paris visit with Vincent Bolloré and convinced Hollande of conciliation; the €45.7M-equivalent investment announced 2011 (the quai plus railways to Niger, "où jamais un train n'a circulé," per Bolloré's own speech); the Hollande-era ostracism acknowledged by BAL's Senegalese chair Racine Sy after the 2013 Dakar deal ("notre retour est un symbole," after Wade had given the 2006 container terminal to DP World).

## 3.6 The historical canon: Elf, Karachi, Clearstream, Bettencourt, HSBC

**Elf (1989–2003).** France's state oil company as slush fund: €305 million siphoned in four years through the "caisse noire" of the company's "commercial diplomacy." The 1994 COB (predecessor of the AMF) report opened it; judge Eva Joly's information judiciaire of August 18, 1994 ran for nine years. The trial record's own language, preserved: "au grand minimum 5 millions à chaque grand parti"; daily contact with the Élysée; the RPR-weighted caisse noire and Mitterrand's instruction to "rééquilibrer"; Bongo's €16.7M/yr and the 100-million-franc-CFA cash withdrawals; "le lobbying sans argent n'existe pas"; the Cresson/SISIE three-million-franc payment never pursued; the Kohl/Pfahls German tentacle. The November 12, 2003 verdict: Loïk Le Floch-Prigent five years/€375,000; Alfred Sirven five years/€1M (dead in prison February 12, 2005, mid-appeal); André Tarallo four years/€2M — never collected, per Le Monde's audit of the Trésor's blind spot in fine collection ("trop clinquantes vitrines"); €180M in civil damages; €18.6M in fines. Joly's own intimidation record preserved: tapped phone, nailed death threats, doubled protection she found more menacing than reassuring. **The Taiwan-frigate artery ran alongside:** Thomson's €2.5bn contract, €780M of commissions, the secret-défense wall, judge Jean-Pierre's Fabius naming, the ~10 suspicious deaths including DGSE officer Thierry Imbot's April 2000 "fall." The canonical closing fact: no politician was ever definitively convicted in the whole affair.

**Karachi (1994–2002).** Three Agosta-class submarines to Pakistan (€825–850M) and the Sawari II frigates to Saudi Arabia (~€3bn), concluded under prime minister Édouard Balladur, with Nicolas Sarkozy as budget minister and campaign manager. The commission architecture was legal until 2000 ("frais commerciaux exceptionnels" — bribes to foreign officials were deductible), but *retro-commissions* — kickbacks back to France — were always criminal. Balladur imposed two Lebanese intermediaries, Ziad Takieddine and Abdul Rahman El-Assir, on DCN International at the last moment; the contract price was marked up so that Pakistani officials took 338 million francs while 216 million francs were added and returned toward France (Libération's 197-page contract scoop). Investigators traced some $111M in commissions, $50M in kickbacks, ~$2M as retro-commissions; judge Marc Trévidic's 2009 finding that the financial track was the only credible one and that the highest levels knew why payments stopped when Chirac halted them in 1995–96. The Cour des comptes validated Balladur's campaign accounts on October 12, 1995, "de haute lutte" against its own rapporteurs, enabling state reimbursement. **The May 8, 2002 bombing** in Karachi killed 11 French DCN engineers (15 dead); judge Jean-Louis Bruguière's al-Qaeda thesis held for years until Trévidic's reprisal thesis: the attack as retaliation for the halted payments. The couriers: Nicolas Bazire (Balladur's chief of staff, now LVMH managing director, Sarkozy's wedding witness) and Thierry Gaubert (Sarkozy's thirty-year friend), both detained in 2011, accused by their ex-wives (Princess Helen of Yugoslavia: "voluminous bags" of Swiss cash; Nicola Johnson, Takieddine's ex-wife, the document source). The judicial ladder: 2009 first convictions; June 2020 six convicted (Takieddine five years); the Cour de justice de la République (the court for ministers) March 4, 2021: Balladur relaxed, François Léotard two years suspended; Paris appeal January 2025 confirming Takieddine's five years, Dominique de Villepin's former chief of staff... the appeal's roster: Donnedieu de Vabres four years/€50,000, Gaubert one year firm commuted/€60,000, El-Assir four years in absentia, Castellan two years — while the victims' families' claims were declared irreceivable and both informations judiciaires (attack and financing) remain formally open. Plus the New Left Review's Villepin nugget — the discreet Élysée unit created in 1995 to hunt the Balladur war chest, and Chirac barring Sarkozy from flying to Riyadh on the eve of the MIKSA signing in 2004.

**The Lafayette/Taiwan money (the parallel artery).** The 1991 contract: six Lafayette frigates at $2.8 billion. The commissions: more than $900M passed through twenty-plus Wang-family accounts frozen in 2001–2003, of which **$138.04M restituted to Taiwan on July 11, 2023**, on a 50–50 sharing basis with Berne. The middleman Andrew Wang died in 2015. The French-side judicial outcome — the Yin Ching-feng murder trigger (the Taiwanese captain about to blow the whistle, murdered December 1993) and decades of French non-prosecution under secret-défense — remains the open leg; judge Jean-Pierre's book, the Fabius naming, and the suspicious deaths are in the record. Le Parisien's October 21, 2003 summary is the anchor quote: "CINQ MILLIARDS de francs (750 millions d'euros) de commissions occultes, huit morts, des enquêtes dans cinq pays, et un secret-défense"; the Swiss judge Paul Perraudin alerted police to death threats during his own inquiry (Le Temps, May 2005); Wikipedia's own summary concedes that "certaines personnes liées à ce dossier ont trouvé la mort d'une manière inexpliquée."

**Clearstream (2004–2011).** The fake listings: in 2004, fabricated Clearstream bank-account listings naming politicians — including Sarkozy and senior Socialists — were fed to investigating judges via General Philippe Rondot, in the context of the Taiwan-frigates commissions. July 27, 2007: Villepin placed under investigation for "complicité de dénonciation calomnieuse"; November 18, 2008: sent to trial with four others. September 23, 2009: the unprecedented — a sitting president, Sarkozy, interviewed on TF1 from New York, declaring the accused "coupables" days before the verdict. The September 21–October 23, 2009 trial: Rondot's eight-hour testimony, the prosecution's requisitions: 18 months suspended for Villepin. January 28, 2010: **relaxé** — acquitted — in a 326-page judgment with Gergorin convicted (three years, 15 firm) and Lahoud (three years, 18 firm). January 29, 2010: the parquet appealed the relaxe — an appeal the magistrates' unions and Franceinfo openly suspected of Élysée pressure, with Villepin calling it "political." The May 2011 re-trial; **September 14, 2011: relaxe confirmed** in a 103-page arrêt that inverted the roles (Lahoud the instigator, Gergorin the "corbeau"). The institutional scar tissue preserved: the subordinated parquet's independence debate; Villepin's unanswered question about a sitting president sitting as partie civile atop the Conseil supérieur de la magistrature; the Bourgi parallel claims on the very eve of the appeal verdict — the Bongo-financed-1988-Le-Pen-campaign claim and the $20M Chirac/Villepin accusations that forced a diffamation counter-suit.

**Bettencourt (2007–2015), both proceedings cleanly separated.** *(1) The Banier-Bettencourt abuse-of-weakness case:* photographer François-Marie Banier convicted at Bordeaux on May 28, 2015 — three years (six suspended), €350,000 fine, €158M in damages; financial adviser Patrice de Maistre 30 months (12 suspended) and €250,000 fine plus €12M damages after his deal with Françoise Bettencourt-Meyers (he renounced appeal); on appeal (August 24, 2016): Banier four years suspended and €375,000, d'Orgeval 18 months suspended, notary Bonduelle relaxed, lawyer Pascal Wilhelm one year suspended plus €3M damages, and the damages collapsed to a symbolic €1 after the 2010 family reconciliation. *(2) The Woerth-Bettencourt campaign-finance and influence-peddling case:* Éric Woerth — treasurer of Sarkozy's 2007 campaign and budget minister — **relaxed on both counts on May 28, 2015**, closing the file that had opened with Claire Thibout's €50k-cash claim against him and Sarkozy, with de Maistre's letters contradicting Woerth's testimony. The counter-scandal preserved: prosecutor Philippe Courroye's illegal surveillance of journalists from Le Monde — the same Courroye whose name recurs in the Clearstream feeding chain.

**HSBC/SwissLeaks (2008–2017).** Falciani's files, handed to French authorities end-2008: 127,000 accounts, 79,000 individuals, 180 nationalities. The French administration's quantification: at least €1.67 billion in sums fraudulently withheld with the bank's help; the SwissLeaks operation of February 2015 (the Le Monde-led ICIJ consortium) revealed €180.6bn transiting the Swiss accounts between November 2006 and March 2007. HSBC Private Bank placed under investigation November 18, 2014. **November 14, 2017: the first-ever CJIP** — €300 million, decomposed as €86.4M disgorgement, €71.6M additional penalty for "minimal cooperation," and €142M state damages — roughly 20% of the fraud, no conviction recorded. The personal residue: ex-CEO Peter Braunwalder's CRPC in January 2019 (one year suspended, €500,000, not entered on the criminal record). The Belgian parallel ~€300M. The comparator ladder: UBS's failed CJIP talks followed by the record €3.7bn February 2019 verdict (appealed); Crédit Suisse ~€238M; Santander €22.5M (December 2025). The systemic insight from the criminal-law literature, preserved verbatim: a convicted bank loses licenses worldwide — which is exactly why the deal was struck. The CJIP table it opened, complete: HSBC €300M (2017); Société Générale €250M (2018, the PNF's second-ever CJIP, the LIA corruption dossier — alongside the bank's US settlements: $275M DOJ + $475M CFTC on Libor, $292M DOJ on Libya, totaling $1.34bn, then the November 2018 $1.34bn sanctions settlement on Cuba/Iran/Sudan and the 2017 ACPR €5M AML blame); Carmignac €30M; Google €500M plus €465M fiscal settlement (September 2019); Airbus €2.08bn (2020, coordinated with the DOJ/SFO — the Libyan CJIP of November 2022 added €15.8M; the Exclusive Networks CJIP of 2025, €16M, was the first triggered by a compliance officer); McDonald's France €508M (2022); Crédit Agricole CIB €88.2M (September 8, 2025, the cum-cum montages); HSBC Bank plc €267.5M (January 8, 2026) — with BNP Paribas, Société Générale and Natixis still targeted on the same intragroup dividend arbitrages, the PNF stating an enjeu of over €4 billion, and the cum-cum macro-frame preserved at €1–3bn/yr cost to the state. Total: ~70 CJIPs, €2bn+ collected, no conviction.

**The banks' American fines (the extraterritorial canon).** BNP Paribas' June 30, 2014 guilty plea and $8.9bn fine for Sudan/Iran/Cuba sanctions violations — decades of wire "stripping" via the Geneva subsidiary, internal alerts since 2005, a one-year dollar-clearing suspension in oil and gas from January 1, 2015, five executives sacrificed including chief operating officer Chodron de Courcel, Hollande's personal intercession with Obama failing, governor Noyer's complaint on dollar hegemony. The 2025 sequel: the October 17, 2025 New York jury verdict finding BNP complicit in Sudanese atrocities ($20.75M to three plaintiffs, 23,000 potential claimants signaled, the stock dropping 8–10.5% and dragging SG and Crédit Agricole; no provision booked; appeal vowed by February 9, 2026). The structural frame: European banks paid ~$16bn in US penalties 2009–2014; Siemens paid $800M in 2008; the Defence ministry's own doctrine paper now cites the BNP case as the textbook of extraterritorial "economic warfare."

## 3.7 The newer files: McKinsey, Fonds Marianne, Uber Files, MoDem, Thales

**McKinsey.** The Senate commission's demolition (report March 16–17, 2022, senators Bazin and Assassi): ministry consulting spend more than doubled from €379.1M to €893.9M (2018–2021, +45% in 2021 alone); strategic consulting nearly tripled from €152.5M to €445.6M; non-IT spend €177M (2014) → €247.5M (2021); the operators' sample (SNCF, La Poste, EDF, RATP — the Big-4's parapublic circuit) €324.2M/yr — over €1 billion with IT in 2021, concentrated in five ministries (Interior, finance, armed forces, ecology, social affairs — the social ministries multiplying strategy spend twentyfold during COVID). The minister's "stable ~€140M" claim dismantled as a partial average excluding IT and operators. The FEACO comparator: France's public consulting market €657M in 2018 versus Germany's €3,143M and the UK's €2,640M — 1.56€ per 1000€ of state opex versus 6.76 UK and 7.20 Germany. The flagship contracts: >€12M for the COVID vaccination campaign; €4M for the APL reform; €1M for a CNAV reorganization study preceding a pension reform that never happened; €500k for an "évolution du métier d'enseignant" study for a colloquium that never occurred. Mais ils pourraient être des alliés utiles pour gagner la France" — Gore-Coty's email); the "kill switch" deployed during police raids; the greyball deception tool. The parliamentary commission (November 2022–July 2023, Danielle Simonnet LFI rapporteuse) concluded July 18, 2023: "des alliés au plus haut niveau de l'État" and "une grande opération de manipulation," explicitly posing the contrepartie question and recording that "certains dirigeants d'Uber ont financé la campagne de M. ## 3.8 The resource files: Kazakhstan, Niger, UraMin, Mozambique

**Kazakhgate.** The October 27, 2010 announcement of the package — 45 EC145 Eurocopter helicopters, an Astrium satellite center, 295 Alstom locomotives (~$2bn) — signed June 27, 2011, ten days after the Belgian penal-transaction law benefited Patokh Chodiev (the Uzbek-born, Belgian-passport oligarch who paid a €23M transaction to escape a corruption conviction rooted in the 1996 Tractebel €25M bribe, while his lawyer Armand De Decker allegedly collected over €700,000). ## 4.2 Party finance: €64.1 million and the rules that shape it

The state's party financing (CNCCFP; the 2026 envelope fixed by décret September 4, 2025 at **€64.1 million**): the first fraction (€30,077,144.37) distributed by votes, the second (€34,148,730.90) by declared parliamentarians. ## 4.13 The rescues: banks, industry, and the state as backstop
